## Method in one sentence

A provotype is a deliberately designed type that **embodies an evidence-backed tension** so stakeholders can collaboratively analyse the present field, expose different stakes and assumptions, and explore alternative design possibilities.

This is an analytical and generative bridge between field investigation and design. It is not simply a controversial concept, a rough prototype or a persuasive device.

Source anchors: physical PDF pp. 1–2 (printed pp. 388–389) and conclusion on physical p. 10 (printed p. 397).

## What the paper contributes

### Theoretical root

The paper traces provotyping to cooperative analysis and Activity Theory. Contradictions inside and between activities are treated as possible drivers of change. The designer makes a selected contradiction experienceable so current practice can be questioned and alternatives can be generated.

Source anchor: physical p. 2 (printed p. 389).

### Four contribution areas

Figure 6 positions provotyping across two questions:

1. Who is primarily being provoked: people in the field or organisational stakeholders?
2. Is the purpose to provoke/analyse or to explore/design?

The resulting overlapping contribution areas are:

- Critical Design;
- collaborative ideation;
- generative design research;
- organisational change.

This is a positioning map, not a rigid four-box classification. A single provotype may operate across more than one area.

Source anchor: physical p. 8, Figure 6 (printed p. 395), visually checked.

### Characteristics of design provocations

The paper identifies three characteristics:

- they embody and reify a chosen tension;
- they require facilitation;
- they can provoke at different moments.

The three moments are:

1. **First encounter** — the initial form, framing and explanation provoke interpretation.
2. **In use** — situated interaction, appropriation and side effects generate reflection.
3. **Upon reflection** — workings or rationale are revealed and prior experience is reinterpreted, opening alternatives.

Source anchors: physical pp. 8–9 (printed pp. 395–396), visually checked.

### Three design guidelines

1. **Balance inconspicuousness and intrusion.** The artefact must be accepted enough to stay in the situation, yet noticeable or estranging enough to interrupt settled perception.
2. **Maintain some mystery, but explain eventually.** Productive uncertainty can invite interpretation; the closing reveal/debrief is necessary so inquiry does not end in confusion or deception.
3. **Provide handles for exploration.** Participants need something they can manipulate, trace, compare, reposition or otherwise follow through curiosity.

Source anchor: physical p. 9 (printed p. 396), visually checked.

## What it is not

| Adjacent approach | Primary emphasis | Difference from provotyping |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional prototype | Concrete product form, function, feasibility or usability | A provotype is used earlier and works more strongly on purpose, context, assumptions and tensions |
| Critical Design | Critical stance toward wider societal values and public debate | Participatory provotyping applies a critical stance instrumentally inside a design process to learn with stakeholders and open a design space |
| Technology probe | Deploy a working technology to study appropriation and future technological potential | Provotyping is explicitly grounded in a selected contradiction and bridges field understanding, design and stakeholder perceptions |
| Boundary object | Shared representation flexible enough for different communities | A provotype also deliberately provokes by embodying tension; shared use alone is not sufficient |
| Usability test | Locate breakdowns in task performance or interface comprehension | Provotyping asks what values, stakes, contradictions and alternatives the encounter makes visible |

Source anchors: physical pp. 2–3 (printed pp. 389–390).

## Operational protocol

### 1. Name the analytical need

State what cannot be learned well through prose-only explanation, interview recall or a preference survey.

### 2. Trace the tension

Write the tension as:

```text
Evidence-backed position / practice A
    ↕ tension or contradiction
Evidence-backed position / practice B
```

Record the evidence for both sides. If one side is an inference, desired future or assumed organisational view, label it accordingly. Do not fabricate symmetry.

### 3. Choose target and purpose

Name:

- participating stakeholder group(s);
- whether the primary purpose is `provoke and analyse` or `explore and design`;
- the abstract product level being explored: intention/purpose, context, principle or detailed form/function.

Provotyping is most useful when the learning question concerns the more abstract levels. If the main question is whether a control works, use a conventional prototype test.

### 4. Materialise the tension

Design an artefact, scenario, rule, interaction, representation or working system in which both sides of the tension can be encountered. It must allow a response beyond agreement with the designer.

Check:

- What does the artefact make difficult to ignore?
- What can participants do with or through it?
- Could a participant reject the framing, reinterpret it or propose an alternative?
- Is the provocation proportionate and ethically acceptable?

### 5. Plan the three moments

| Moment | Researcher action | Evidence to record |
|---|---|---|
| First encounter | Give the minimum framing needed for safe participation; avoid explaining the desired interpretation | Spontaneous reading, questions, discomfort, attraction, rejection, assumed purpose |
| In use | Let participants manipulate, trace, compare or adapt; facilitate without steering toward agreement | Actions, workarounds, references to lived experience, value conflicts, new stakes, side effects |
| Upon reflection | Reveal the workings and design rationale; distinguish source evidence from designer interpretation | What changes after explanation, alternative framings, proposed adaptations, unresolved disagreement |

Never conceal consent, risk, data provenance or synthetic/mock status in the name of productive mystery.

### 6. Facilitate across stakes

Useful neutral prompts include:

- What do you think this is doing?
- What does it assume?
- Where does this fit or conflict with your experience?
- Whose position is visible here, and whose is missing?
- What would you change, refuse or add?
- What does this make possible, and what might it close down?

Do not require consensus. The method is valuable when it makes disagreement and different stakes available for analysis.

### 7. Convert response into knowledge cautiously

Separate the record into:

```text
Observed encounter
→ participant account
→ researcher interpretation
→ tension confirmed / qualified / disputed / missing
→ design-space implication
→ next proposition or test
```

A strong provocation does not validate the proposed solution. It may instead reveal that the framing, artefact or project direction is wrong.

## Evaluation questions

- Was the embodied tension traceable to admitted evidence?
- Did the encounter surface different assumptions, values, stakes or purposes?
- Did it support collaborative analysis rather than only individual reaction?
- Did participants explore alternatives at the intended level?
- Was facilitation necessary, and how did it shape the result?
- What happened at first encounter, in use and after explanation?
- Did intrusion cause productive reflection, simple rejection or harm?
- Did mystery invite sense-making or merely create confusion?
- Were there adequate handles for exploration?
- What was confirmed, qualified, disputed or newly missing?

## Risks and limits

- **Designer overdetermination:** the artefact may encode the researcher's preferred moral answer.
- **Spectacle without inquiry:** visual strangeness may attract attention without exposing a meaningful tension.
- **False participation:** stakeholder reaction may be used to legitimise a decision already made.
- **Facilitation bias:** prompts and explanation can manufacture the apparent insight.
- **Rejection:** an intrusive or estranging artefact may close dialogue rather than open it.
- **Misclassification:** a usability issue or aesthetic preference can be mistaken for a value conflict.
- **Overclaiming:** provoked discussion is analytical input, not proof of adoption, effectiveness or systemic change.

The source paper reports a bounded indoor-climate case involving two ten-day family deployments and project-partner engagements. Its guidelines are presented as developing rather than exhaustive. Transfer to another context therefore requires a new rationale and testing.
