# Semester 1 Taught-Method Catalog

Scope: methods taught across Design Fundamentals, Core Research Methods and Studio practice.

## Status and use

This is a reusable record of taught methodological capability, not a secondary-research evidence bank. Course PDFs are not bundled in this repository. The course filenames and physical page anchors below are source locators; verify the original page before relying on an exact quotation, diagram or definition.

## Core methods

| Method | Course anchor | What it structures | Main output | Important limit | Application note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Telling–Making–Enacting | `Engage Fundamental`, p. 27 | Selects narrative, generative and embodied methods according to what each can reveal and miss | Multi-mode engagement or test plan | No single mode reveals behaviour, tacit knowledge and meaning together | Match the mode to the kind of knowledge sought |
| Engagement strategy spectrum | `Engage Fundamental`, p. 26 | Positions engagement by duration and by extractive/informing versus mutual input, learning and decision-making | Engagement rationale | Attendance alone does not make an activity participatory | State the intended degree of participant influence |
| Reflexive sensemaking | `Engage Fundamental`, pp. 42–44 | Moves from context, tensions and assets to insights while naming positionality, ambiguity and unintended consequences | Insight plus limitations and reflexive memo | Reflection is not descriptive evidence | Keep interpretation visible and bounded |
| Design Ethnography | `Design Ethnography`, pp. 18–35 | Integrates observation, fieldnotes, interviews, ethics and triangulation | Contextual observation and interpretation record | Small qualitative samples are not generalisable | Make researcher position and context explicit |
| Descriptive / Reflective Fieldnotes | Same source, pp. 25–26 | Separates factual, behavioural and sensory description from questions and interpretation | Two-column fieldnote record | Reflection remains interpretation | Record the context and researcher position |
| Participant Observation Protocol | Same source, p. 22 | Access, ethics, observer role, protocol, rapport, description/interpretation, withdrawal and timely notes | Observation protocol | Use only where ethically approved and relevant | Decide whether observation is necessary before collecting it |
| Interview method selection | Same source, pp. 29–30 | Distinguishes structured, semi-structured and unstructured interviews and their trade-offs | Interview rationale and question structure | Interviews explain meaning but are subjective and non-generalisable | Match structure to the question and evidence limits |
| Ethics and triangulation gate | Same source, p. 34 | Requires ethical approval, participation rationale, support and complementary data | Pre-test ethics and method-combination check | Triangulation does not remove bias automatically | Treat ethics as a design condition, not paperwork after the fact |
| Participatory evidence integration | `Pre-read for class`, especially pp. 3–15 | Uses prior evidence and theory as inputs while allowing local validation or dispute | Iterated, locally challenged evidence chain | Prior evidence can lead participants | Preserve critique and agenda-setting |
| Visual Mapping principles | `Visual Mapping`, pp. 26–38; principles p. 27 | Selects elements, accommodates variables/time, exposes groupings and manages scale | Purpose-specific visual map | Visual proximity is not proof of causality | State the question and data behind the map |
| Iterative Processes | `ITERATE Presentation`, pp. 16–20, 51–68 | Revisits research and observations through successive versions | Versioned learning and reframing record | Repetition without a learning question is not meaningful iteration | Record what each version was meant to learn |

## Supporting methods

| Method | Course anchor | Use | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mapping and sorting for insight | `Slides-SecondaryResearch`, pp. 20–30 | Organise sources/themes, inspect connections and identify possibilities or challenges | Do not turn course content into topic evidence |
| Storytelling in Design | `Storytelling`, pp. 5–6 | Visualise events from a protagonist's perspective and the evolution of an experience | Check whose perspective is missing |
| Narrative iteration | `ITERATE Presentation`, pp. 54–68 | Re-map a story, shift viewpoint, prototype “what if” narratives and question absence | Treat a narrative as a proposition, not a fact |
| Journey / process mapping | `Visual Mapping`, p. 30 | Represent sequence or passage through a process | Do not imply a settled service journey without evidence |
| Network / stakeholder mapping | Same source, p. 31 | Represent actors, things and relational position | Do not infer motivation or causal power from layout |
| Asset mapping | Same source, p. 32 | Identify strengths, relationships and resources | Avoid deficit-only framing |
| Thematic / mind mapping | Same source, p. 33 | Group and connect themes | Use another map when time, hierarchy or causality must remain explicit |
| Experience mapping | Same source, p. 34 | Make situated experience legible | State whose experience is represented |

## Semester relationship

```text
Semester 1: engage, observe, record, synthesise and iterate responsibly
Semester 2: analyse systems, transitions, change mechanisms and experiments
```

Use the smallest combination that answers the project's analytical need. Neither semester's methods turn an interpretation into a validated claim by themselves.
